## Why Japan's emergency systems confuse foreigners

| Confusion | Reality |
| --- | --- |
| **119** vs **110** order | Different services, not one universal line |
| US **911** habit | Japan uses separate numbers |
| Loud **phone alerts** in Japanese | Normal; learn basic alert types |
| Small **earthquakes** daily in some regions | Most are harmless shakes |
| Anime disaster imagery | Daily life is calm infrastructure |

Foreign residents do fine with a short cheat sheet on the phone and one prepared bag at home.

## Emergency numbers explained

| Number | Service | Call when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **110** | **Police** | Crime, serious accident, immediate danger, lost passport theft in progress |
| **119** | **Fire and ambulance** | Fire, serious injury, sudden illness needing transport |

Both are **free** from mobile phones, including foreign SIMs.

### 110 (police)

- Report crimes and traffic accidents with injuries or disputes needing police
- Ask for English: say **"English please"** or **"Eigo onegaishimasu"**
- Tokyo Metropolitan Police and some prefectures offer limited English support

### 119 (ambulance / fire)

- **Ambulance** for medical emergencies (chest pain, unconsciousness, severe injury)
- **Fire** for fires, gas leaks, rescue needs
- Dispatcher may ask which service; stay calm and answer location first

<InfoBox title="Foreign residents panic about this">
  Calling 119 for a non-emergency blocks real cases. Use clinics for fever and minor issues. Use 119 when life or limb is at risk.
</InfoBox>

## Police vs ambulance vs fire services

| Situation | Number |
| --- | --- |
| Wallet stolen yesterday | 110 (non-urgent desk) or visit koban |
| Chest pain now | 119 ambulance |
| Apartment fire smell | 119 |
| Neighbor noise dispute | Police non-emergency line or ward office, not 119 |
| Heatstroke collapse | 119 |
| Lost child in mall | 110 or staff |

**Koban** (local police box) handles minor lost items and directions. Not for medical emergencies.

## Earthquake realities in daily life

Japan records many small earthquakes. Most cause **no damage**.

| Intensity | What residents feel |
| --- | --- |
| Weak shake | Lamp sway, continue if safe |
| Strong shake | Drop, cover, hold on; check gas |
| Major event | Follow alerts, evacuate if instructed |

**At home habits:**

- Know **off** positions for gas stove
- **Shoes** by bed in earthquake-prone regions
- **Heavy furniture** anchored if landlord allows
- **Meeting point** with household

After shaking: check **NHK**, municipal app, and phone alerts before assuming the worst.

## Typhoon and heavy rain preparedness

Typhoon season peaks roughly **August to October**. Rain bands can flood low areas and stop trains.

| Before typhoon | Action |
| --- | --- |
| Charge phone and power bank | Comms |
| Buy water and food | 2-3 days mindset |
| Clear balcony | Flying items |
| Know **nearest evacuation zone** | Ward map |
| Tape or close shutters | If instructed |

## Tsunami warnings explained simply

**Tsunami** waves follow offshore earthquakes. Coastal areas get **specific evacuation orders**.

| Alert level mindset | Action |
| --- | --- |
| **Warning** | Move inland or to high ground per map |
| **Advisory** | Stay alert, avoid coast |
| Inland Tokyo apartment | Often not a tsunami zone; follow local map |

Ward office and **disaster hazard maps** (*hazard map*) show tsunami and flood zones. Pick up a paper copy or PDF from [city hall guide](/residents/japan-city-hall-guide).

## Emergency alerts on Japanese phones

**J-Alert** and similar systems send **loud notifications** to phones in affected areas.

| Alert type | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Earthquake early warning** | Shake coming; take cover |
| **Tsunami** | Coastal evacuation |
| **Missile / national test** | Rare; follow government text |
| **Heavy rain / landslide** | Avoid rivers and slopes |

iPhone and Android show **English titles** on some alerts. Full text may be Japanese.

**Tip:** Do not disable all emergency alerts. Disable only non-critical app noise separately.

## Convenience stores during emergencies

**Konbini** (7-Eleven, Lawson, FamilyMart) often stay open when supermarkets close.

| Item | Why |
| --- | --- |
| Water, food | Quick restock |
| Batteries, tape | Useful |
| Toilet access | Some allow with purchase |
| ATM | Cash if networks busy |

They are backup, not a full disaster plan.

## Emergency kits for residents

**Starter kit** (one backpack per adult):

| Item | Qty mindset |
| --- | --- |
| Water | 2-3 liters |
| Non-perishable food | 3 days snack level |
| Flashlight | 1 |
| Batteries | Set |
| Cash in small bills | ¥10,000-¥20,000 |
| Copies of ID | Residence card, passport |
| Medications | 1 week if prescribed |
| Phone cable and power bank | 1 |
| Rain poncho | 1 |
| Whistle | 1 |
| Mask | Several |
| Plastic bags | Few |

Add **winter layer** if you live in Hokkaido or Tohoku.

## Language barrier expectations

| Service | English |
| --- | --- |
| 110 / 119 | Variable; location first |
| Hospital ER in cities | Some English staff |
| Shelters | Volunteers may help |
| Alerts | Mixed English on phones |

Prepare a **wallet card** in Japanese:

- Name, address, blood type if known
- Allergies and medications
- Emergency contact phone
- Insurance number

## Shelter systems explained

**Evacuation shelters** (*hinanjo*) open in schools and public halls during major events.

| Type | Purpose |
| --- | --- |
| **Temporary shelter** | Short stay in crisis |
| **Evacuation site** | Safe gathering point |
| **Welfare shelter** | Longer support if home uninhabitable |

Register with ward **disaster volunteer** lists if you want community updates. Foreign residents can use shelters same as neighbors.

## Common mistakes foreign residents make

- Calling **119** for minor fever
- Ignoring **phone alerts** then missing evacuation
- **No water** at home because "konbini is near"
- Running outside during **earthquake** (falling glass risk)
- Using **elevators** during quake or fire
- Not knowing **ward hazard map**
- Forgetting **insurance card** in wallet

## What is exaggerated online about disasters

<InfoBox title="Foreign residents panic about this">
  - **"Tokyo will collapse tomorrow"** headlines: Japan plans seriously; daily risk for most residents is routine small shakes.
  - **Every quake needs evacuation**: False. Follow intensity and official orders.
  - **You must speak perfect Japanese to survive**: False. Numbers, apps, and neighbors help.
  - **Stores empty for weeks**: Typhoons may clear shelves 1-2 days; restock is fast in cities.
  - **Foreigners excluded from shelters**: Not normal policy.
</InfoBox>

Preparation is **layered calm**, not bunker mentality.

## FAQ

### Can I call 110 or 119 without Japanese?

Yes. Give location in English and say you need English support.

### Is 119 ambulance free?

Transport is low cost but not always zero. Hospital treatment uses insurance.

### What is the earthquake app everyone uses?

Yurekuru Call is popular. Municipal apps add local shelter info.

### Should I run outside during a quake?

Usually no. Drop, cover, hold on indoors away from windows.

### Do I need a go bag in Tokyo?

A small kit is wise everywhere in Japan.

### Will trains run after a typhoon?

Often partial service next day after safety checks.

### Where is my tsunami zone?

Check ward hazard map. Inland wards may only face flooding, not tsunami.

### Are disaster drills mandatory?

Workplaces and schools drill. Apartment drills vary.

### Does NHI cover emergency room?

Yes with patient share. Bring insurance card.

### What if I lose power?

Use phone alerts on battery saver. Konbini may stay open nearby.

## What makes restaurants in Japan different

Japan dining runs on clear routines more than long conversations. Many places optimize turnover, show menus with photos, and expect you to read floor stickers and counter signs without hand-holding.

- Staff may greet you loudly when you enter. A small nod is enough.
- Solo diners are common at counters, ramen shops, and chain restaurants.
- Volume stays low. Your table is part of the room, not a private booth.
- The bill is usually one total. Split checks are rare outside large chains.

<TipBox title="Field note">
  When you feel lost, watch one local party from door to payment. Copy their queue position, ticket handoff, and exit line before you improvise.
</TipBox>

## Entering the restaurant and waiting to be seated

Read the door first. Stickers may show no shoes, no large bags, cash only, or ticket machines inside. If a host is present, give your party size with fingers or say the number softly.

- Follow floor tape and wait off the doorway so traffic can pass.
- Some shops ask you to write party size on a clipboard before seating.
- Counter spots may seat you in arrival order even when tables look empty.
- If the room is full, wait outside the mat line until staff wave you in.

<InfoBox title="Watch for this">
  Do not move chairs, partitions, or coat hooks unless staff invite you. Fixed layouts keep aisles clear for trays and other guests.
</InfoBox>

## Ticket machines and ordering before you sit

Ramen shops, curry spots, and many fast casual places use vending-style ticket machines near the entrance. You pay before you sit, then hand the printed slip to staff.

- Insert cash or tap card only if the machine shows that method.
- Tap the photo or button for your set. Take every slip the machine prints.
- Step aside after buying so the next person can reach the machine.
- If the screen freezes, watch one cycle or point at the plastic food model you want.

<TipBox>
  Keep small bills for older machines. Change trays sit beside the slot; staff rarely have time to break large notes during a rush.
</TipBox>

## Ordering at the table

After you sit, ordering may be verbal, paper menu, tablet, or QR code. Family chains often have English on tablets. Traditional spots rely on photos and set codes.

- Match the set number on the menu photo when Japanese text wins.
- Use **Sumimasen** at moderate volume to call staff for help or allergy questions.
- Send tablet orders once per round; do not spam the send button.
- Say **Osusume** if you want a safe staff pick when the counter is calm.

## Water, tea, towels, and service expectations

Cold water often arrives free without asking. Hot tea may replace water at sushi or set-meal spots. Carafes on the table mean you pour for yourself.

- Wet towels (**oshibori**) are for hands before eating, not your face or phone.
- Refills are not automatic everywhere. Raise a hand if you wait a long time.
- Clearing empty plates is staff work. Stack lightly if you must, but do not bus your own table unless it is a self-return format.
- Service is steady and quiet. You are not ignored when staff skip small talk.

## Chopsticks, shared dishes, and table manners

Place chopsticks on the rest or tray, not upright in rice. Pass shared plates with clean ends or let others take first if the dish is communal.

- Lift small bowls to drink soup or rice when it is easier than leaning.
- Slurping noodles is acceptable. Talking on speakerphone is not.
- Smoking areas exist in some izakaya zones. Follow posted rules and seat labels.
- Allergies and dietary limits need a simple phrase or allergy card. Do not assume kitchens can swap ingredients on busy nights.

<InfoBox title="Watch for this">
  Blowing your nose at the table is avoided. Excuse yourself to the restroom if you need to.
</InfoBox>

## Paying the bill

Payment timing depends on the format. Casual shops want you at the register with a slip. Cafes and some formal meals bring the terminal to the table.

- Watch locals: if everyone queues at the door with paper, join that line after you stand.
- Say **Okaikei onegaishimasu** when you want the check at sit-down spots.
- Have your wallet ready before you reach the register so the line moves.
- Bag coins off to the side if you pay cash so the next guest is not blocked.

## Cash, cards, IC cards, and QR payments

Cities accept tap-to-pay widely, but small kitchens and legacy bars may post cash-only signs. Read payment stickers at the ticket machine before you commit.

| Method | Where it works | Tourist tip |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cash | Almost everywhere | Keep coins and smaller bills for machines and rural stops |
| Credit / debit tap | Chains, many city restaurants | Check foreign card fees before you rely on tap |
| Mobile Suica / PASMO | Some chains and convenience partners | Top up before dinner rush |
| QR wallets | Selected chains | Look for logos at the register, not on every mom-and-pop shop |

## Tipping rules in Japan

Tipping is not expected and can confuse staff. Service is included in the price. A clear thank you and calm exit are the norm.

- Do not leave coins on the table unless a specific tourist-facing sign says otherwise.
- If staff chase you with forgotten money, it is usually change, not a tip request.
- High-end hospitality may add a service line on the bill. That is not a prompt for extra cash.

## Common restaurant types tourists should know

| Type | What to expect | Good for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ramen | Ticket machine, fast turnover, counter seats | Quick solo meals |
| Izakaya | Shared plates, drink rounds, louder evenings | Groups and late dinners |
| Teishoku | Set lunch with rice, soup, pickles | Balanced midday meals |
| Family chains | Tablets, allergens, predictable English | First nights when you are tired |
| Kaiten sushi | Plates by color, total by stack | Easy ordering by sight |
| Konbini | No real seating, eat elsewhere | Late trains and tight schedules |

## Common mistakes tourists make

- Blocking the ticket machine while you decode the menu
- Speaking loudly across the room when staff are two meters away
- Assuming every shop splits bills or takes every foreign card
- Sticking chopsticks upright in a rice bowl
- Treating the wet towel like a napkin for the table
- Lingering at ramen counters after you finish when a line waits outside

<TipBox title="Field note">
  One fix per meal beats trying to memorize every rule. Clear the line, control volume, and pay the way locals queue.
</TipBox>

## Simple restaurant checklist

- Read door signs for tickets, cash, shoes, and bags
- Buy machine tickets before you sit when required
- Keep voice low and acknowledge staff greetings
- Use photos and set numbers when language fails
- Confirm payment spot before you stand up
- Carry some cash even when you plan to tap

## Final advice

Let predictable formats do the heavy lifting. Ticket rows, tablet photos, and plastic food models exist so you can eat well without fluent Japanese.

Pick one courtesy habit per meal: do not block flows, match room volume, and pay where the shop expects. Calm beats perfection every time.

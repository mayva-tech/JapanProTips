## Why tourists love Japanese drugstores

Drugstores here are **dense, bright, and organized**. You can solve three trip problems in one stop: a blister, a heat-rash cream, and a gift bag of face masks.

- **Huge skincare and beauty rows** at prices that feel fair for the quality.
- **OTC medicine** with pictograms for symptoms (cough, fever, stomach).
- **English on many tourist-area tags**, not guaranteed in rural towns.
- **Long hours** in city centers, often 9:00-22:00 or later.
- **Tax-free counters** in busy districts when you qualify as a short-term visitor.

They are not a clinic. Staff can orient you, but **self-diagnosis limits** still apply.

## Difference between drugstores and pharmacies

| | Drugstore (**kusuriya** / chain shop) | Pharmacy (**yakkyoku**, licensed) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Main floor | Cosmetics, snacks, household goods, some OTC | Medicine focus |
| Pharmacist | On duty for questions, not always on every aisle | Required for prescription role |
| Prescriptions | Usually separate counter or partner clinic | Filled here |
| Tourist shopping | Skincare, masks, vitamins, OTC | When you need real pharmacy advice |
| Signage | Matsumoto Kiyoshi, Welcia, Sundrug logos | Green cross, **yakkyoku** kanji |

**Rule of thumb:** Buy sunscreen and lip balm like a shop. Treat **strong cold packs and unfamiliar kanji medicine** like a pharmacy decision: read labels, ask staff, or skip if unsure.

## Popular chains explained

### Matsumoto Kiyoshi

- Yellow and pink branding tourists recognize instantly.
- Flagship stores in Shibuya, Shinjuku, and Kyoto station areas.
- Strong **cosmetics wall** and tourist tax-free desks in central Tokyo.

### Sundrug

- Red signage, similar layout to other chains.
- Often slightly different sale cycles. Compare sunscreen prices if you have time.

### Welcia

- Common in neighborhoods and stations, not only downtown cores.
- Good for **everyday refill** when you are not at a tourist mega-store.

### Don Quijote pharmacy sections

- Don Quijote (**Donki**) mixes discount chaos with drugstore aisles.
- Fun for souvenirs plus sunscreen at midnight.
- Lines can be long. Tax-free desk rules still apply.

<TipBox title="Practical shopping tip">
  Same product, different sale: check one chain near your hotel before you bulk-buy ten masks at a station flagship.
</TipBox>

## What tourists usually buy

### Cold medicine

- Combo packs for fever, cough, and runny nose (Japanese formulas differ from Western brands you know).
- Read **day vs night** labels. Drowsy formulas and driving do not mix.

### Pain relievers

- Tablets for headache and muscle pain. Dosage units may differ from home labels.
- Avoid stacking with medicine you brought from abroad without checking interactions.

### Masks

- Surgical, fashion, pollen, and humidifier-style masks.
- Great souvenirs. Light to pack.

### Skincare

- Sunscreen (**biore**, **Anessa**, **Skin Aqua** lines appear everywhere).
- Cleansers, toners, sheet masks, lip balms.

### Beauty products

- Makeup removers, eyelash tools, hair masks.
- Test one item before you buy six duplicates for friends.

### Vitamins

- Drink shots near the register, multi-vitamin packs, collagen drinks (treat as food supplement, not miracle cure).

### Eye drops

- FX, Santen, and Rohto-style cooling drops tourists love on video.
- Contact lens users: check **soft lens safe** labels.

| Category | Tourist fit | Caution |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sunscreen | High | Check SPF and PA labels |
| Sheet masks | High | Bulky in luggage |
| Cold combo packs | Medium | Read drowsy warnings |
| Strong pain gels | Medium | Quantity limits for export |
| Prescription-only items | N/A | Not on open shelves |

## Prescription vs non-prescription medicine

**Over-the-counter (OTC)** products sit on open shelves with category labels. Strength tiers exist in Japan. Some items foreigners expect OTC may be **behind the counter** or prescription-only here.

**Prescription medicine** requires a Japanese doctor visit and a pharmacy that fills scripts. Tourists on short trips rarely use this path unless they already have local care.

<InfoBox title="Tourists are surprised by">
  Staff sometimes walk you to the pharmacy counter for a product you thought was self-serve. That usually means the item needs a brief check or is not sold without guidance.
</InfoBox>

## Medicine restrictions tourists should know

- You are responsible for **import rules at home** when you fly back. Some ingredients allowed in Japan are restricted elsewhere.
- **Pseudoephedrine** and certain cold formulas face tight rules in multiple countries. Check your destination customs site.
- Do not mail medicine abroad without checking postal rules.
- Buying **large quantities** of the same product can look like resale. Buy personal-use amounts.
- Bringing medicine **into** Japan: personal use quantities are usually fine; declare if customs asks.

When in doubt, buy one box, keep receipts, and skip gifts of unknown pills.

## Tax-free shopping rules

Tax-free (**menzei**) desks appear in tourist-heavy stores. Typical pattern:

1. Same-day purchase at participating store.
2. Passport required (physical or digital per store policy).
3. Minimum spend (often **¥5,000** on eligible goods in one receipt, rules vary).
4. Staff attach receipt to passport page or scan in system.
5. Do not open sealed consumables in ways that void the stamp before exit.

| Step | What to have ready |
| --- | --- |
| Passport | Original or accepted digital copy |
| Payment | Card or cash as store allows |
| Time | Counter queues at peak hours |
| Packing | Unopened goods until airport if rules require |

Rules change. Ask the counter each time. For broader payment context see [money and cards in Japan](/guides/money-payments-japan). Mall and electronics rules are in the [tax-free shopping guide](/guides/japan-tax-free-shopping).

## Japanese skincare culture explained

Skincare here emphasizes **sun protection**, gentle cleansing, and layering light products. PA ratings on sunscreen matter for UVA. You will see:

- **Double cleanse** culture (oil cleanser plus face wash).
- **Hydrating toners** (lotion) used generously.
- **Sheet masks** as affordable treats, not medical treatment.
- **Whitening** labels often mean **brightening / spot care**, not skin color change. Read ingredients if that wording confuses you.

Drugstores sell entry-level lines and premium lines side by side. Price does not always track quality. Start mid-shelf unless you already know a product from home.

## How pharmacists may interact with customers

Licensed staff may ask short questions through translation apps:

- Symptoms and how long you felt sick
- Allergies or other medicine you take
- Age and pregnancy (some products restricted)

They might recommend a **different product** than the one you pointed at. That is normal professional caution, not upselling.

<TipBox title="Practical shopping tip">
  Open your phone notes with **symptoms in simple English**, plus any drug allergies written clearly. Photos of your home medicine labels help.
</TipBox>

## What tourists get confused about

<InfoBox title="Tourists are surprised by">
  - **Everything looks OTC** until it is not. Counter-only items exist.
  - **Kanji ingredient lists** with no English on smaller boxes.
  - **Heat patches** (**kairo**) sold next to medicine but treated like general goods.
  - **Drinkable vitamins** marketed like energy drinks.
  - Tax-free lines that wrap around the register while you still need one more item.
  - Store staff saying no to a product you saw on TikTok because your use case does not fit.
</InfoBox>

## Translation and ingredient tips

- Use camera translate on the **drug facts panel**, not only the front marketing side.
- Look for pictograms: fever, throat, stomach, itch.
- **Salicylic acid, ibuprofen, acetaminophen** names may appear in katakana.
- Patch tests for skincare: buy one sheet mask, not a carton, if your skin is reactive.
- Keep a photo of ingredients if you have known allergies.

## Drugstore etiquette

- Line up at the register. Basket on belt when asked.
- Do not open sealed tester boxes unless marked **tester**.
- Ask before you photograph staff faces at the pharmacy counter.
- Speak quietly. Stores can be crowded but not loud.
- Put used tester wipes in trash if provided.
- If you decline a bag, say so before they bag automatically (eco moves vary by chain).

## Common mistakes tourists make

- Buying **three boxes of the same cold medicine** without reading duplicate ingredients.
- Mixing **Japanese OTC** with home prescriptions without professional advice.
- Assuming **Don Quijote** prices beat every drugstore (sometimes yes, sometimes no).
- Forgetting passport at hotel on tax-free day.
- Packing **full-size sunscreen bottles** in carry-on without checking liquid limits.
- Treating **eye drops** like party tricks on a dry flight (follow label limits).
- Shopping while visibly sick without a mask in crowded aisles (courtesy norm, see [public etiquette](/guides/how-to-use-japanese-toilets)).

## FAQ

### Can tourists buy medicine in Japan?

Yes, many **OTC** items are sold in drugstores. Some items need pharmacy guidance. Prescription drugs require a local prescription.

### Is Matsumoto Kiyoshi tax-free?

Many central stores offer tax-free service for qualifying short-term visitors. Ask at the counter with passport.

### Are Japanese drugstores cheaper than home?

Often competitive on skincare and masks. Compare unit prices. Sales rotate weekly.

### Can I bring medicine back on the plane?

Usually personal quantities if your home country allows the ingredients. Check customs rules.

### Do staff speak English?

Tourist areas: sometimes basic English or translation tablets. Rural stores: less common.

### Is sunscreen in Japan better?

Formulas target high UV and humidity. PA rating matters. Buy for your skin type, not hype.

### Can I use credit cards?

Yes in most urban chains. Carry cash backup.

### What is the green cross sign?

Often marks a **pharmacy** or medicine-focused section. Not every green sign means full Western-style pharmacy services.

### Are collagen drinks medicine?

Sold as **foods / supplements**. Not cures. Read labels like snacks with claims.

### Can I return opened skincare?

Usually no on opened cosmetics. Check store policy before you test extensively.

## How healthcare works in Japan

Japan mixes public insurance with private clinics and hospitals. You choose a front-door clinic for everyday issues; hospitals handle referrals, tests, and emergencies within their scope.

- Everyone enrolled in a valid plan pays a patient share at the visit.
- Fees follow national schedules, so similar visits cost similar amounts.
- Records stay with clinics; bring prior paperwork when you switch doctors.
- Prevention and chronic care usually stay at small clinics near home.

<InfoBox title="Watch for this">
  Travel insurance alone does not replace resident National Health Insurance or workplace coverage for daily care after you register in Japan.
</InfoBox>

## Clinics vs hospitals

**Clinics** (クリニック / 診療所) are small offices: internal medicine, ENT, dermatology, orthopedics, and more. **Hospitals** (病院) are larger facilities with departments, imaging, and inpatient beds.

| Setting | Typical role | First visit for a cold? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Clinic | Routine care, prescriptions, referrals | Usually yes |
| Hospital | Specialists, ER, surgery, inpatient | Only if clinic directs you or emergency |

- Hospital departments may require a referral slip from a clinic.
- Some hospitals run evening urgent clinics without full ER chaos.
- Names can confuse: a "university hospital" is large; a "internal medicine clinic" is not.

## When to visit a clinic first

Start at a **clinic near your home** for fever, rashes, mild injuries, stomach issues, and medication refills.

- Clinics know local patients and keep visit history.
- Same-day slots exist at many internal medicine offices.
- Orthopedic clinics handle sprains; eye clinics handle conjunctivitis.
- If symptoms worsen, the clinic writes a referral (紹介状) to a hospital.

<TipBox title="Field note">
  Search maps for your ward name plus specialty in Japanese once, save two clinics with good hours, and pin them before you are sick.
</TipBox>

## Health insurance basics

Residents use **National Health Insurance (NHI)** or **workplace insurance (shakai hoken)**. Both cover insured care with a copay, often around 30% at the desk for many visits.

- Show your insurance card at reception every time.
- Premiums and cards tie to your registered address.
- Uninsured visits cost full price and create paperwork later.
- See the NHI guide for enrollment, workplace switches, and card replacement.

## What to bring to appointments

Reception asks for ID and payment every visit, even repeat ones.

### Health insurance card

Your **insurance card** (保険証 or My Number health card where issued) proves enrollment.

- Expired cards need renewal at city hall or through HR.
- Digital versions depend on local rollout; carry physical backup until staff confirm.

### Residence card

**Residence card** confirms identity and status when clinics file records.

- Address should match insurance records when possible.
- Passport backup helps if staff photocopy ID.

### Cash or payment card

Many clinics accept cash and cards; smaller offices may prefer cash.

- Keep coins for lockers and vending drinks.
- Ask at reception if you need pay-at-machine kiosks after the visit.

## Appointment systems in Japan

Booking styles vary: phone, web form, LINE accounts, and in-person same-day tickets.

- First visits may be longer; say **sho-kai** (first visit) when booking.
- Cancel early if you cannot come; no-show policies exist.
- Some specialties book weeks out; internal medicine is often faster.
- Workplace checkups use separate employer schedules.

## Walk-in visits and waiting times

**Walk-in** (飛び込み) works at many clinics with a numbered ticket system.

- Take a reception slip or machine ticket at the door.
- Morning slots fill on Monday and after holidays.
- Bring a book; waits of an hour are normal at busy clinics.
- Evening clinics exist but may cost slightly more on some schedules.

## Pharmacies and prescriptions

Doctors issue **prescriptions** (処方箋) you fill at pharmacies, often separate from the clinic building.

- **Dispensing pharmacies** (調剤薬局) prepare medicine from the slip.
- Explain allergies and other drugs you take; phrase lists help.
- One-week bundles are common; refills need a new visit unless chronic care is set.
- OTC medicine at drugstores does not replace prescribed antibiotics.

## Emergency rooms and ambulances

**Emergency departments** handle severe chest pain, major trauma, stroke symptoms, and other acute crises. **Ambulance** (119) is free to call; you still pay hospital care costs with insurance.

- Mild fever usually does not belong in ER; use clinics or night urgent centers.
- Bring insurance card and ID if you can; someone can follow with them.
- Night pediatric lines exist in cities; check your ward website once.
- Translation help varies; bring a friend for complex intake.

<InfoBox>
  If you are unsure whether to call 119, many prefectures publish simple decision charts online. When breathing is hard or consciousness changes, call without delay.
</InfoBox>

## English-speaking medical support

English-ready clinics cluster in large cities; suburban offices may be Japanese-only with picture boards.

- Hospital international desks exist at major centers.
- Translation apps help intake forms; write symptoms in Japanese keywords when possible.
- Embassy lists and ward foreign resident sections publish clinic directories.
- Friend accompaniment is normal and accepted.

## Typical costs and payments

Costs depend on insurance, tests, and facility type. You pay the copay at checkout unless a billing desk sends a later invoice.

| Visit type | What affects price |
| --- | --- |
| Simple clinic consult | Copay on exam fee |
| Blood tests / imaging | Added test lines |
| Hospital specialist | Referral plus facility fee |
| Pharmacy | Separate copay on drugs |

- High-cost care can trigger refunds through municipal limits; ask city hall if bills spike.
- Receipts (領収書) help tax and reimbursement paperwork.

## Common mistakes foreign residents make

- Going straight to a large hospital ER for a routine cold
- Forgetting insurance card and paying full fare, then chasing refunds
- Losing prescription slips before reaching the pharmacy
- Assuming drugstore staff can prescribe antibiotics
- Skipping follow-up because symptoms improved halfway through a course

## Simple healthcare checklist

- Enroll in NHI or confirm workplace insurance active
- Pin one internal medicine clinic and one hospital near home
- Pack insurance card, residence card, and payment in one pouch
- Know pharmacy hours near your clinic
- Save ward foreign-resident health page and 119 on your phone
- Photograph prescription and diagnosis paper for records

## Final advice

Use clinics as your default door, hospitals when scale or urgency requires it, and pharmacies as a separate stop. The system rewards calm triage more than dramatic detours.

Keep insurance current after every move. Reception desks see expired cards every week, and fixing enrollment later is slower than showing the right card on day one.
